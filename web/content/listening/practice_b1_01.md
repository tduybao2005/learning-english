NARRATOR: Section 4. You will hear a careers adviser giving a talk on writing a good CV. First, you have some time to look at questions 31 to 40.
[PAUSE:25]
F: Good afternoon, everyone, and thank you for coming to today's session on CV writing. My name is Peter, and I've worked as a careers adviser for over fifteen years, helping graduates find their first job. Let's start with the basics. Your CV should ideally be no longer than two pages, especially if you're early in your career, since recruiters often spend less than a minute on the first read. Always start with a short personal statement, no more than three or four sentences, summarising who you are and what you're looking for. Your work experience should be listed in reverse chronological order, meaning your most recent job comes first. For each role, focus on your achievements rather than simply listing your duties — use numbers where possible, such as "increased sales by fifteen percent," rather than vague statements. Skills sections are useful, but be honest — only include skills you can genuinely demonstrate if asked in an interview. When it comes to formatting, keep it simple and consistent: choose one font, use clear headings, and avoid unnecessary colours or graphics, especially for traditional industries. Always proofread carefully, and ideally ask someone else to check it too, since spelling mistakes create a poor first impression. Tailor your CV for each application — read the job advert carefully and highlight the experience most relevant to that specific role. Don't forget your contact details at the top, including a professional email address, ideally based on your name rather than a nickname. References are usually not needed on the CV itself; simply write "references available on request" if you wish. Finally, remember that your CV is just the first step — it needs to be strong enough to get you an interview, where you can then explain your experience in more depth. If you'd like feedback on your own CV, I'll be available after this talk, and you can also book a one-to-one session through the careers office website. Beyond the CV itself, it's worth thinking about your online presence too — many recruiters now check LinkedIn profiles before even reading your application, so make sure yours is up to date and consistent with your CV. A professional photo, a clear summary, and a few endorsements from previous colleagues or lecturers can make a real difference. When you're describing your achievements, try to use strong action verbs — words like "led," "developed," or "coordinated" sound far more confident than passive phrases like "was responsible for." It's also worth keeping a separate document listing every achievement, project, and piece of feedback you receive throughout your studies or career, even small ones, because it's much easier to update your CV from a running list than to try to remember everything from scratch every time you apply for a job. Don't underestimate the value of volunteer work or extracurricular activities either — organising a university society, for example, demonstrates leadership and organisation just as much as paid work does. If you're struggling to fit everything onto two pages, prioritise ruthlessly: keep only what's relevant to the specific job you're applying for, and save the rest for your LinkedIn profile or a longer portfolio. Thank you all for listening, and good luck with your applications.
