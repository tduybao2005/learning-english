NARRATOR: Section 4. You will hear a nutritionist giving a talk about sugar in everyday food. First, you have some time to look at questions 31 to 40.
[PAUSE:25]
F: Good evening, everyone, and thank you for coming to tonight's talk on sugar in our everyday diet. My name is Claire, and I'm a qualified nutritionist working with the local health authority. Tonight, I want to talk about something that affects almost everyone, whether we realise it or not: hidden sugar in the food we eat. Most of us know that sweets, cakes, and fizzy drinks contain sugar, but far fewer people realise how much sugar is hidden in everyday products like bread, pasta sauces, and even some savoury snacks. The World Health Organization recommends that added sugar should make up no more than ten percent of our daily calorie intake, and ideally less than five percent for the best health benefits. For an average adult, that works out to roughly thirty grams a day, which is about seven teaspoons. However, many people in this country consume double or even triple that amount without realising it. One of the biggest sources of hidden sugar is flavoured yoghurt, which can contain as much sugar as a chocolate bar, despite being marketed as a healthy option. Similarly, many breakfast cereals aimed at children contain far more sugar than people expect, sometimes more than a third of the weight of the cereal itself. Learning to read food labels is one of the most useful skills you can develop. Look for the word "sugar" on the ingredients list — if it appears near the top, the product likely contains a significant amount. Also watch out for other names for sugar, such as glucose syrup, fructose, or dextrose, which food companies sometimes use to make sugar content less obvious. A simple and effective habit is to compare similar products side by side and choose the one with less sugar per hundred grams. Reducing sugar gradually, rather than cutting it out suddenly, tends to be more successful, since your taste buds slowly adjust to less sweetness over a few weeks. Drinking water instead of sugary drinks is probably the single most effective change most people can make, since fizzy drinks and juices are often the largest source of sugar in a typical diet. I'd also encourage you to cook more meals from scratch when you can, since this gives you full control over how much sugar goes into your food. None of this means you can never enjoy a treat — moderation, not perfection, is really the goal here. One area I haven't mentioned yet is sugar in so-called "healthy" snack bars, which are often marketed towards people trying to eat well or lose weight. Many of these bars contain as much sugar as a standard chocolate bar, simply dressed up with words like "natural" or "energy" on the packaging, so it's worth checking the label just as carefully as you would with any sweet treat. Another area worth mentioning is children's packed lunches — a small change, like swapping a fruit yoghurt for plain yoghurt with fresh fruit added yourself, can make a meaningful difference over a school year without the child noticing much of a taste change. I also want to touch briefly on sugar in drinks specifically aimed at sport and exercise, since these are often marketed as healthy but can contain surprisingly high amounts of sugar, more suited to elite athletes doing intense, prolonged exercise than to someone doing a casual gym session. If you do enjoy a sweet treat now and then, portion size matters more than most people realise — a smaller portion enjoyed slowly is often more satisfying than a large one eaten quickly out of habit. Thank you all for listening, and I'm happy to take any questions now.
