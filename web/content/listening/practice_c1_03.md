NARRATOR: Section 4. You will hear part of a lecture on the psychology of music. First, you have some time to look at questions 31 to 35.

F: Good afternoon, everyone. Today's lecture explores a question that has fascinated psychologists, and indeed philosophers, for centuries: why does music, an abstract arrangement of pitch and rhythm carrying no inherent semantic meaning, so reliably produce powerful emotional responses in listeners across virtually every documented human culture? Let's begin with what's sometimes called the expectation-violation theory, most closely associated with the music theorist Leonard Meyer, whose influential nineteen fifty-six work argued that musical emotion arises substantially from the interplay between expectation and surprise. According to this account, as we listen to music, our brains continuously generate implicit predictions about what's likely to come next, based on the statistical regularities of the musical style we're familiar with, and emotional responses arise, at least in part, from whether those predictions are confirmed, delayed, or violated, a mechanism with intriguing parallels to broader theories of prediction and surprise within cognitive neuroscience more generally. A delayed resolution of a musical phrase, for instance, tends to generate tension, while an unexpected but ultimately satisfying resolution can produce a distinct sense of pleasure, sometimes measurably correlated with dopamine release in brain imaging studies, not dissimilar to the reward pathways activated by other pleasurable stimuli entirely unrelated to music. It's worth pausing to consider why this predictive mechanism might have evolved in the first place, given that music, unlike language or spatial reasoning, doesn't obviously confer any direct survival advantage of the kind evolutionary psychologists typically look for when explaining a widespread cognitive capacity. Several competing explanations have been proposed over the years. One influential account suggests that our capacity for musical prediction is essentially a byproduct of more general auditory prediction mechanisms that evolved for genuinely adaptive purposes, such as tracking environmental sounds, interpreting vocal intonation for social and emotional cues, and rapidly detecting unexpected, potentially threatening changes in our acoustic environment, with music essentially co-opting and playfully exercising these pre-existing predictive circuits rather than representing a distinct evolutionary adaptation in its own right. An alternative, though not necessarily incompatible, account emphasises music's role in social bonding, proposing that shared musical experiences, particularly synchronised group activities like communal singing, dancing, or drumming, may have conferred genuine adaptive advantages by strengthening group cohesion and coordination among early human communities, advantages that would have been particularly valuable in a species as fundamentally social and cooperative as our own. Both accounts, it's worth noting, remain somewhat speculative, given the obvious methodological difficulty of directly testing hypotheses about behaviours and cognitive capacities that emerged deep in our evolutionary past, though converging evidence from developmental psychology, cross-cultural anthropology, and comparative studies of musical or proto-musical behaviour in other species has gradually lent increasing plausibility to some version of the social bonding account in particular.
NARRATOR: We now continue with the second half of the lecture, covering questions 36 to 40.
[PAUSE:15]
F: This expectation-based account, however compelling, doesn't fully explain everything we observe, and it's worth considering some complementary perspectives. One important addition comes from research on what's termed "chills" or frisson, that distinctive physical sensation, often described as shivers down the spine, that certain musical passages reliably produce in a substantial proportion of listeners. Neuroimaging studies of this phenomenon have found it engages brain regions associated with reward and pleasure remarkably similar to those activated by food, sex, and certain drugs, suggesting music may, at least for some listeners, be tapping into genuinely ancient reward circuitry, rather than representing some entirely separate, culturally specific phenomenon unique to aesthetic experience. A further complicating factor concerns the substantial role of individual difference and cultural context, which any complete theory of musical emotion must ultimately accommodate. What triggers frisson, or indeed any strong emotional response, in one listener may leave another entirely unmoved, and cross-cultural research has demonstrated convincingly that certain musical features assumed to be universal, such as the association between minor keys and sadness, are considerably more culturally specific than earlier Western-centric research initially assumed, with listeners from cultures without extensive exposure to Western tonal music frequently failing to replicate this particular association at anything resembling the strength found in Western populations. This has led contemporary researchers towards a more integrated model, one that treats musical emotion as arising from a complex interaction between innate, likely evolutionarily ancient perceptual and reward mechanisms on one hand, and substantial learned, culturally specific associations on the other, a synthesis that, while considerably more complex than Meyer's original framework, arguably offers a more empirically defensible account of why music moves us so profoundly, and so variably, across the extraordinary diversity of human musical traditions. It's also worth briefly noting the methodological challenges researchers face in studying this topic empirically, since self-reported emotional responses to music, while easy to collect, are notoriously unreliable compared with physiological measures like heart rate, skin conductance, or neuroimaging, which don't rely on a listener's ability to accurately introspect on and verbally articulate what is often quite a fleeting, subtle internal state. I want to close today by briefly considering the practical implications of this research, since it isn't purely an abstract, academic question. Music therapy, an increasingly evidence-based clinical discipline, draws directly on many of the mechanisms we've discussed today, using carefully structured musical interventions to support outcomes ranging from emotional regulation in patients with dementia, where familiar music from a patient's youth can reliably evoke strong autobiographical memories even in advanced stages of cognitive decline, to motor rehabilitation following stroke, where rhythmic auditory cueing has been shown to measurably improve gait and movement coordination through mechanisms closely related to the predictive, expectation-based processes we discussed earlier in relation to Meyer's theory. There are also increasingly sophisticated commercial applications worth mentioning briefly, from algorithmically generated adaptive soundtracks in video games, which respond dynamically to in-game events precisely by exploiting expectation and surprise, to the careful curation of background music in retail environments, explicitly designed to influence consumer mood and, ultimately, purchasing behaviour, which raises its own set of ethical questions not entirely dissimilar to those we discussed a fortnight ago regarding nudge-based choice architecture, questions I'd encourage you to consider further as you read this week's assigned articles ahead of our next seminar.
