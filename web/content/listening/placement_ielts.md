NARRATOR: Section 4. You will hear part of a lecture given by a professor on the history and development of coral reef ecosystems. First, you have some time to look at questions 31 to 40.
[PAUSE:20]
C: Good morning, everyone. Today I want to continue our unit on marine ecosystems by looking specifically at coral reefs — how they form, why they matter, and what threats they currently face.
Let's start with the basics. Coral reefs are built by tiny animals called coral polyps. Each polyp secretes a hard skeleton made of calcium carbonate, and over time, millions of these skeletons join together to form the structure we recognise as a reef.
It's important to understand that reefs grow extremely slowly. On average, a healthy reef grows only about one centimetre per year, which means that some of the largest reef systems in the world, like the Great Barrier Reef, took thousands of years to reach their current size.
Now, coral polyps have a fascinating relationship with a type of algae called zooxanthellae. This algae lives inside the coral's tissue and provides it with food through photosynthesis, in exchange for a safe, sunlit home. This partnership is called a symbiotic relationship, and it's essential for reef survival, because without the algae, corals cannot get enough energy to grow or reproduce.
This relationship, however, is also the reef's greatest vulnerability. When ocean temperatures rise even slightly above normal, the coral becomes stressed and expels the algae living inside it. Because the algae also gives coral its colour, the coral turns completely white, a process known as coral bleaching.
Bleached coral isn't dead immediately, but if the stressful conditions continue for more than a few weeks, the coral will eventually starve and die. Recovery is possible if temperatures return to normal quickly enough, but repeated bleaching events give reefs less and less time to recover.
Beyond temperature, reefs face several other threats. Ocean acidification, caused by the ocean absorbing excess carbon dioxide, makes it harder for coral polyps to form their calcium carbonate skeletons in the first place. Pollution from agricultural runoff, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilisers, causes algae blooms that block sunlight from reaching the coral. And of course, physical damage from fishing practices, anchors, and coastal development continues to destroy reef structures directly.
Despite these threats, reefs remain incredibly important, both ecologically and economically. Ecologically, reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, yet they support around twenty-five percent of all marine species, making them one of the most biodiverse ecosystems on the planet.
Economically, reefs provide enormous value through fishing, tourism, and coastal protection. One recent estimate places the global economic value of coral reefs at around three hundred and seventy-five billion dollars per year.
So what is being done to protect them? One major approach is the creation of marine protected areas, where fishing and development are restricted or banned entirely. Studies show that reefs inside well-managed protected areas recover from bleaching events roughly twice as fast as unprotected reefs nearby.
Scientists are also experimenting with coral restoration techniques, such as growing coral fragments in underwater nurseries before transplanting them onto damaged reefs. This method has shown promising results, though it remains expensive and difficult to scale up to the size needed to make a global difference.
Another promising avenue of research involves identifying and breeding coral strains that are naturally more resistant to heat stress. Researchers at several marine institutes have already identified some coral populations that survive bleaching events better than others, and are studying the genetic basis for this resilience.
Finally, addressing climate change at a global level remains the single most important long-term solution, since local protection measures alone cannot prevent rising ocean temperatures. Without significant reductions in global carbon emissions, scientists estimate that the majority of the world's coral reefs could disappear within the next fifty years.
In our next lecture, we'll examine specific case studies of reef restoration projects in Southeast Asia and the Caribbean, so please read the assigned chapter before then.
